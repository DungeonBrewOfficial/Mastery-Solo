# The Solo Game — Reconstruction Record

*A restoration of the first game Jon Boydston ever built: a solo/two-player wilderness campaign played on hand-drawn graph paper, circa 1998–2001. The original rules existed in a notebook in 12-year-old handwriting and are lost. This document rebuilds them by the method that has already worked twice: propose a plausible structure, let the witness object, record the corrections.*

*Every claim carries a tag. **[A]** attested — Jon remembers it. **[I]** inferred — the source books make it nearly certain. **[C]** conjectured — a guess, marked as mine, standing only until corrected. The tags are the point: a restoration that hides where the new stones are is a forgery.*

---

## 1. The Shelf

What was physically owned, because the whole design grew around what wasn't:

**[A]** D&D Basic Set, Mentzer revision (1983, "red box") — Players Manual and Dungeon Masters Rulebook. Read cover to cover, repeatedly, on the school bus.

**[A]** Players Companion: Book One (1984, from the Companion box) — also read cover to cover. Equipment "stolen" from its lists.

**[A]** *Not owned:* the Expert Set (levels 4–14, the wilderness rules, stronghold construction). *Not owned:* Dungeon Master's Companion: Book Two (War Machine, dominion DM rules, stronghold construction costs).

**[A]** Dating: the Savage Garden bus-ride era puts active play around 1998–2001.

**[A-b]** The non-TSR shelf. The brother's testimony (secondhand, via Jon, 2026-06-11 — tagged **[A-b]** throughout) adds Risk to the household game shelf, alongside the already-attested FF1 on NES and the Monopoly-class roll-to-move convention. The source material was never just the D&D books; it was everything within reach of the living room floor.

Source PDFs now on file: *Set 1 — Basic Rules Box Set* (124 pp., both booklets; DM's Rulebook begins ~p. 73, monster section ~p. 93, treasure ~p. 111) and *Set 3 — Companion Rules Box Set* (105 pp.; Book One is pp. 4–37, Book Two begins ~p. 38 — Book Two is outside the attested shelf and is cited here only to mark what Jon *didn't* have).

## 2. The Bridge Thesis

The organizing claim of this reconstruction:

The shelf held levels 1–3 (Basic) and levels 15–25 (Companion) with nothing between. Every system Jon invented sits exactly inside that gap, where the Expert Set should have been. No wilderness rules → a hand-drawn terrain map, a hand-sorted bestiary, and board-game roll-to-move. No progression past level 3 → a purchasable equipment ladder that let a permanently low-level character keep growing. Castle rules visible at level 15+ but unreachable → the stronghold pulled forward and made the point of the game. Even the castle layer's *unfinished* state maps onto the shelf: Book One supplied the stronghold concept and the staff wages, but the construction cost tables were in Book Two, which he didn't own. The expansion stalled at the missing table.

The game was not house-ruled D&D. It was a board-game overworld wrapped around a red-box dungeon engine — load-bearing improvisation everywhere Expert should have been. Jon's own words, repurposed: he couldn't bridge the gap between Basic and Companion, so the game *was* the bridge.

**Falsifiability note:** if any attested invention turns out to sit *outside* the Expert-shaped hole, the neat version of this thesis breaks. None has yet.

**Amendment (first hit taken):** the brother's testimony that combat may have been settled with Risk-style d6s rather than d20-vs-AC places a board-game part squarely *inside* territory the red box covered. If true, the neat version is dead and a broader principle replaces it: board-game parts didn't just fill what the shelf was missing — they also replaced whatever the books made slow. The attack matrices require an open rulebook and a table lookup per swing; Risk dice resolve from the fist, at floor level, mid-sprawl. The unifying rule of the design stops being "bridge the gap" and becomes "keep the loop moving." The Expert-shaped hole still explains *most* of the inventions; it no longer explains all of them. See §4a.

## 3. The Attested Record

What the witness remembers, in his words where possible.

**The map.** Hand-drawn on 1/8-inch graph paper, 6 or 9 sheets taped together (~100 squares on a long edge per sheet). Terrain drawn deliberately and meticulously: mountains, forests, swamps, deserts. Progress tracked by penciling little Xs square by square and erasing them behind you. **[A]**

**Encounters.** Monsters taken from the back of the red box Dungeon Masters Rulebook and organized by terrain and HD — his own labor, since the entries carry no terrain field (see §4). Entering terrain triggered a roll to see what monster was fought. **[A]** The HD sort may have filtered encounters down to roughly 1 HD monsters in every environment — hedged: "that may not be strictly true." **[A, hedged]**

**Movement.** Dice were rolled to determine how far you moved. **[A]** (No such rule exists anywhere in D&D — this is the board-game gene. Die size unknown; see §5.)

**Characters.** The seven red box classes: Fighter, Cleric, Magic-User, Thief, Elf, Dwarf, Halfling. **[A]** Characters never reached high level. **[A]** No memory of any character dying, and no remembered death rule. **[A]**

**Combat.** Mostly 1v1; monster attacks rolled straight, as the book directs. **[A]**

**Loot.** Treasure determined by looking up the treasure tables (the Treasure Type letters in the DM's Rulebook). **[A]** The loop: kill things, get gold, buy the next thing. **[A]**

**The equipment ladder.** The full equipment list — red box inventory plus items stolen from Companion Book One (which introduced the bastard sword, heavy crossbow, bola, blowgun, scale armor, among others) — was hand-duplicated in a notebook at each of five metal tiers: Iron → Steel → Silver → Gold → Mithril. Hundreds of line items, handwritten. Jon's own assessment, which this document adopts: the metal tiers are notationally identical to the book's +1/+2/+3 weapon hierarchy — "the idea that a Gold Broadsword is superior to a Steel Broadsword is the same notion that adding a +2 to a Broadsword makes it better." The difference is economic, not mathematical: the book's hierarchy assumed scarcity (magic is found), Jon's assumed commerce (everything has a price tag). **[A]**

*Revision:* what the tiers **did** is now testified to be unrecovered, and possibly nothing. "I remember writing them. I remember pricing them. I don't remember what they did" — and no memory of play extending much beyond "wander and kill stuff." **[A]** The catalog is therefore attested as a *built* artifact, not a *played* mechanic. See §3a.

### 3a. Built versus Played

A distinction the record now needs, because the evidence has produced it three times. The game-as-played was small: start at your corner, roll to move, roll for what happens, fight it Risk-style, take the treasure roll, wander on. The game-as-built was large: six taped sheets of meticulous terrain, a hand-sorted bestiary, a five-metal equipment catalog priced line by line, a stronghold expansion drafted from Companion Book One and never finished. The built layer exceeded the played layer by an order of magnitude, and the witness's memory tracks the distinction — he remembers *making* the catalog vividly and cannot say whether it was ever much used.

This is not a defect of the artifact; it is the artifact. TSR itself published far more game than any table ever ran — weapon speed factors and dominion economies sat unplayed in books everywhere, and nobody called D&D unfinished. The Solo Game replicated the full shape of the hobby at miniature scale: a small living loop inside a large built world, where the building itself was a primary pleasure of play. The reconstruction must therefore restore *two* things and mark the seam between them: the rules as plausibly played, which fit on a page, and the built penumbra, which was most of the love. **[I]**

**The base.** "More of just a base, honestly" — a place to return to, not a refined construction system. A stronghold layer was started, drawing on Book One's stronghold section (printed p. 8), and never finished. **[A]**

**Two-player mode.** His brother ran his own character on the same map, killing monsters alongside. No remembered shared-resource rules. **[A]** The brother adds: each player started at *opposite sides of the map*. **[A-b]** Separate starting corners imply separate home bases and parallel rather than party-based play — closer to two solo games sharing one world than to a cooperative expedition. Whether the two characters ever interacted mechanically remains unrecovered.

**The turn (brother's account).** One die to move. One die to determine "what happened there." **[A-b]** This confirms roll-to-move as a single-die mechanic and refines the encounter procedure: an event roll at the destination, per stop rather than per square. Note the breadth of the phrasing — "what happened there," not "what attacked." Jon's own memory ("roll to see the type of monster I had to fight") may describe the most common result of that die rather than its only result. Held open: whether the event die had non-combat outcomes.

**Combat resolution (contested).** The brother *thinks* encounters were settled with Risk-style d6s, "rather than AC and stuff." **[A-b, hedged]** This directly conflicts with Jon's recollection of rolling monster attacks "straightforward" — itself hedged ("probably mostly"). Both versions are recorded; neither is promoted. See §4a.

## 4. Inferred from the Books

Where the red box ran the game, the red box *is* the rule. The reconstruction assumes the following were played as printed, because the witness describes playing a "B/E character" straight:

**Combat mechanics.** ~~d20 attack rolls against descending AC via the attack matrices, damage by weapon die, initiative, morale on 2d6 with per-monster scores, the five saving throw categories.~~ **[I → contested]** *Revision note, seams showing: this inference rested on the assumption that the red box engine ran as printed wherever it existed. The brother's Risk-dice testimony breaks that assumption. Downgraded; see §4a. The original inference stands only as the* ceiling *of what combat could have been — the books in hand — not as what it was.*

### 4a. The Combat Question (open, two witnesses in conflict)

Jon: attacks "probably mostly" rolled straightforward, 1v1. The brother: Risk-style d6 contests, "rather than AC and stuff." Possibilities, none promoted:

**(i) Two phases.** The game evolved. A fast Risk-dice variant when the brother played (he "played a little"); fuller D&D combat in solo play. Both witnesses remember the version they sat through. This reconciles everything and is suspiciously convenient.

**(ii) The brother is right.** Combat was Risk dice throughout, and Jon's memory is contaminated by the books he owned and read obsessively — he remembers the system he *knew*, not the one he *ran*. Supporting evidence, and it's strong: Jon sorted the bestiary by terrain *and HD*. In d20 combat, HD is a bookkeeping stat a kid barely touches. But if HD set the *number of dice a monster threw* in a Risk-style contest, HD becomes the single most important number in the game — exactly the stat you'd organize your world by. The HD sort is hard to explain under book combat and nearly automatic under dice-pool combat.

**(iii) Jon is right.** It was d20-vs-AC, and the brother's memory is contaminated by Risk itself — the other game on the same floor. Risk dice are a famously sticky memory (the colored cubes, the tie rule); they could have migrated into his recollection from adjacent afternoons.

What would discriminate: whether anyone remembers the attack tables being open during fights; whether AC meant anything; whether "HP" or "armies/hits" feels right for what monsters lost. Not to be interrogated out of either witness — left for recognition.

**Update:** Jon independently proposed HD-as-dice-count to the brother in their own conversation — but on reflection rejected dice-pool scaling ("I don't think we were chucking fistfuls of d6s"). Leading reconstruction, per Jon, "most likely": **a straight Risk port — PC throws 3 dice, monster throws 2, always.** Monster difficulty lived in HP, not dice: harder monsters took longer to kill, and longer fights leak more losses back at the player. **[A, hedged]** This build has the attacker's edge baked in — the player is favored in every exchange — which makes routine farming safe, death rare (retreat always available, as in Risk), and big-HP monsters dangerous by attrition rather than burst. The dragon still works: not a bigger fist, just a fight too long to survive. Jon also allows that the Risk mechanism may have been *the brother's contribution*, adopted into the game — a sibling collaboration. **[A, hedged]** Still unrecovered: what the metal tiers mechanically did under this combat (extra hits removed per won exchange? pip bonuses? armor absorbing losses?) — the shop loop demands they did *something*.

**Gold as experience.** 1 gp recovered = 1 XP, and treasure as the dominant XP source — printed red box rule (DM's Rulebook, treasure section: "Wealth... is worth experience points"). This matters structurally: the remembered loop "get gold and buy shit" was simultaneously the leveling engine, by the book's own design. One currency fed gear, XP, and (aspirationally) the castle. **[I]**

**The level ceiling.** Basic covers levels 1–3 and the shelf offered no legal path past 3. "Never leveled that high" is therefore overdetermined: the books capped the campaign. This is also the likely structural pressure behind the equipment ladder (progression had to live somewhere) and possibly behind the ~1 HD encounter filter (permanently low-level characters can't graduate to ogres). **[I]**

**The terrain sort was interpretive.** Verified against the scans: red box monster stat blocks list AC, HD, Move, Attacks, Damage, No. Appearing, Save As, Morale, Treasure Type, Alignment, XP — no habitat field. Terrain assignments could only come from reading each prose description and making a ruling. The sort was design work, performed monster by monster. Consequence for restoration: his tables can be approximately re-derived by applying the same readings to the same descriptions, and the places where the book gives no hint mark exactly where he had to make a call. **[I]**

**The stronghold layer's ceiling.** Book One gives ownership rules ("purchased, built, or seized"), the staff roster and wages (Seneschal, Chief Magistrate 2,000 gp/month, Reeve 500 gp/month...). Construction costs appear only in Book Two. The unfinished castle expansion therefore had book support for *running* a stronghold and none for *pricing* one. **[I]**

## 5. Conjectures Awaiting Correction

Mine, flagged as mine, ordered by how load-bearing they are. The method: each will eventually be proposed concretely, and the witness's objections are the data.

**The movement die.** ~~A single d6 is the most probable roll-to-move.~~ Upgraded: one die to move, per the brother. **[A-b]** Die size still presumed d6 (Risk and Monopoly put d6s in every fist in that house). **[C on the size only]**

**The encounter trigger.** Refined by the brother's account: one die rolled at the destination to determine "what happened there" — per stop, not per square. **[A-b]** Whether that die could produce outcomes other than a monster (nothing? a find? terrain-specific results?) is open. Jon's "roll to see the type of monster" may simply describe the usual outcome. **[C]**

**The metal tier math.** If the tiers shadow the +1 to +5 hierarchy, each step plausibly added +1 to hit and/or damage over the listed base weapon. Whether armor tiers stepped AC the same way, and what the price multiplier per tier was, is unrecovered. The notebook duplicated *prices* as well as names — what those prices were is one of the genuinely lost things. **[C]**

**The dragon question.** The red box bestiary includes dragons. Whether they made it onto the terrain tables — whether the "chance of a dragon when I wasn't ready" memory reflects his game or bleeds in from later play — is unresolved. The ~1 HD filter, if strict, argues no. **[C]**

**Death.** No remembered rule and no remembered death. Either fights filtered to 1 HD made death vanishingly rare (the boring, therefore likely, answer), or there was an unwritten kid-rule reset. **[C]**

**The castle's function in play.** Given "more of just a base": a fixed home square — rest, shopping, the notebook's shop inventory — rather than a construction minigame. The building ambition existed (the stalled expansion) but the *played* game likely treated the base as a location, not a system. **[C]**

## 6. Where the Record Is Thinnest

Not an interrogation list — these resolve through the propose-and-correct method, not through demands on memory: the movement die and trigger procedure; the tier pricing scheme in the notebook; what a session actually looked like minute to minute (one expedition out and back? open-ended wandering?); whether the brother's character and Jon's ever interacted mechanically at all.
