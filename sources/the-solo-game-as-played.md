# THE SOLO GAME
### as played, c. 1998–2001 — restored 2026

*This is a restoration, not an improvement. It is allowed to be boring. The original was played by one or two brothers on six taped sheets of 1/8-inch graph paper, and the rules below are what the evidence — two witnesses and three source books — says actually happened at floor level. Claims resting on conjecture rather than testimony are marked °, and itemized in the ledger at the end. Everything else is attested.*

---

## PART I — THE GAME AS PLAYED

*(the whole thing; it fits on a page; that is the finding)*

**You need:** the hand-drawn terrain map, a character, d6s, the red box Dungeon Masters Rulebook (monsters and treasure tables), the notebook.

**Your character** is made from the D&D Basic Set, by the book: one of the seven classes — Fighter, Cleric, Magic-User, Thief, Elf, Dwarf, Halfling — rolled and equipped as the Players Manual directs. The book runs levels 1–3; nobody ever got past that, and the game didn't need them to.

**Setup.** Each player starts at his own corner of the map, opposite the other. Solo, you simply start at yours. Your corner is your base — a home square to come back to. It was never much more than that.

**The turn.**
1. **Roll a die. Move that many squares** across the map. The terrain you stop in — plains, forest, swamp, desert, mountains — is where the turn happens.
2. **Roll a die to see what happened there.** Usually° what happened was a monster. The terrain decides which list you roll on; the lists were hand-sorted from the back of the red box DM's Rulebook, by terrain and by Hit Dice, easy monsters in easy country (the re-derived tables are in *bestiary-terrain-sort.md*).
3. **Fight it, Risk-style.** You throw 3 dice, the monster throws 2°. Compare the highest dice, then the next pair°; each comparison lost costs the loser hit points°. The monster's HP comes from its Hit Dice — harder monsters aren't better fighters, they just take longer to kill, and long fights leak damage back at you. You can stop fighting and walk away, like pulling out of a battle in Risk°. That is why nobody ever died: the math favored the attacker 3 to 2, and the door was always open.
4. **Take the treasure.** Look up the monster's Treasure Type letter in the DM's Rulebook and roll what it guarded. Gold counts as experience, because the red box says so — one piece, one point.
5. **Wander on.** That's the game. Two players never needed rules for meeting, because the map was six sheets wide and each was working out from his own corner.

**Spending.** Gold went to the notebook: the equipment catalog, every item priced, in five ascending metals — Iron, Steel, Silver, Gold, Mithril. You went out with the iron set to afford the steel set. What the metals mechanically did — whether a steel sword rolled, hit, or protected any differently — is unrecovered, and the honest answer may be *nothing*°. The buying was the point. The writing-down was the point.

**Death.** No rule survives because no death survives. Between the attacker's edge, the open door, and plains stocked with half-HD chaff, the question apparently never forced itself.°

## PART II — THE GAME AS BUILT

*The played loop above ran inside a much larger built world. The building was not preparation for the game; it was the other half of the game.*

**The map.** Six (possibly nine) sheets of 1/8-inch graph paper, taped, roughly 100 squares to a long edge per sheet. Terrain drawn by hand, deliberately: mountains, forests, swamps, deserts, and the plains between. Travel marked with little penciled Xs, erased behind you as you went. The map is lost. It is the single part of the artifact this restoration makes no attempt to reproduce.

**The bestiary sort.** Every monster in the red box DM's Rulebook, assigned a home terrain by a boy reading prose descriptions on a school bus — the book has no terrain field; every assignment was a ruling. The labor reproduced, in miniature, the Expert Set's wilderness tables, which he did not know existed. One feature came free from the book itself: the dragon entry's color table assigns black to swamp, green to forest, blue to desert, red to mountain. Each deep terrain held its dragon, placed there by Mentzer and feared accordingly.

**The catalog.** The full equipment list of the Basic Set, augmented with arms stolen from the Players Companion (bastard sword, heavy crossbow, bola, blowgun, scale armor), duplicated five times over in 12-year-old handwriting — once per metal — and priced, line by line. Hundreds of entries. The five metals are the book's +1 to +5 weapon hierarchy translated into a commercial economy: what D&D made findable, the Solo Game made *purchasable*, because the loop ran on shopping. Attested as written and priced; not attested as ever much used. A supplement, in the truest TSR tradition: more game than any table would ever run.

**The castle.** An expansion, begun and never finished, sourced from the Strongholds section of Players Companion Book One (printed page 8) — which supplied the dream and the staff payroll but not the construction prices; those were in the Companion DM's book, which the shelf didn't hold. The expansion stalled exactly where the bookshelf ended. In play, the base remained what it had always been: the square you came home to.

**The shelf the game grew from.** D&D Basic Set (Mentzer, red box), read cover to cover on the bus. Players Companion Book One, likewise. No Expert Set — and the game's entire wilderness layer, its movement, its tables, and its gear-driven progression are improvised exactly where Expert would have been. Risk, for the combat engine — possibly the brother's contribution, which would make the Solo Game a two-designer studio. Final Fantasy 1, advisor on monster placement. The design principle unifying every borrowing: *keep the loop moving.*

## Conjecture Ledger (the ° marks)

1. *"Usually a monster"* — the event die's non-monster outcomes, if any, are unrecovered. Brother's phrasing ("what happened there") leaves room; Jon's memory ("what monster I had to fight") fills it with teeth.
2. *Monster throws 2 dice* — "PC got 3, monster got 2" is Jon's stated most-likely; tagged because it's a reconstruction both witnesses endorse but neither remembers performing.
3. *Second-pair comparison and ties to the defender* — imported with the Risk port; unattested in detail.
4. *Hit points lost per losing comparison* — amount unknown. One point per loss is the minimal kid-rule; weapon damage dice are possible but unattested.
5. *Free retreat* — structural inference from Risk plus the no-death record, not a remembered rule.
6. *The inert catalog* — "I don't remember what they did" is testimony of absence, not absence of function. If the metals did anything, the likeliest candidates are pip bonuses or extra hits per won exchange. Unrecovered.
7. *No death rule* — absence of memory from both witnesses, plus structure. A house resurrection rule could have existed and left no trace.

## PHASE TWO (noted, not begun)

*"Build what it was and then we can, maybe, build what my little 12 year old brain imagined it was."*

The restoration above is what it was. What it was imagined to be is a different artifact — the game where the metals matter, the castle gets built, the dragon gets fought on purpose, and the wandering has somewhere to arrive. That game was never written down because it didn't need to be; it ran continuously in the only engine that had it spec'd. Designing it is not restoration and will not pretend to be — it is the commission the 12-year-old left behind.

When and if: it starts from this page, changes nothing attested without saying so, and answers to one constraint already on file — *bland doesn't mean bad, it just means simple.*
